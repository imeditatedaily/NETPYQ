# Cognitive Psychology: Sensation, Perception, Attention, Memory, Intelligence, Emotion

> **At a glance** — attributes of sensation: **quality, intensity, duration, extensity, clearness** (not "quantity") · perception: **Gestalt** laws; **top-down** vs bottom-up · attention: **information-processing** view, limited capacity · memory stages: **encoding → storage → retrieval** · Atkinson–Shiffrin: sensory (iconic, echoic) → short-term → long-term · declarative = **episodic + semantic**; implicit = **non-declarative** · intelligence: **Gardner** (multiple), **Sternberg** (triarchic), **Ceci** (bioecological), **Anderson** (cognitive development) · emotion: **James–Lange**, **Cannon–Bard**

The syllabus: "Cognitive psychology: sensation, perception, attention, memory, learning." Learning theories are in the first chapter (*Psychology: nature, schools, methods and behaviourism*).

## 1. Sensation

**Sensation** (संवेदना) is the detection of physical energy by the sense organs and its conversion into nerve impulses (**transduction**). It is the raw input; **perception** is its interpretation.

**Attributes of sensation** (Titchener's classical list): **quality** (kind: red, sweet), **intensity** (strength), **duration** (how long), **extensity** (spatial spread) and **clearness / vividness** (how much attention it gets). "**Quantity**" is not an attribute; how strong a sensation is falls under intensity. [[PYQ: YOGA-2024JUN-P2-Q078]] Some books add **local sign** (where on the body).

**Psychophysics** [[LIKELY]]:
- **Absolute threshold** — the smallest stimulus detected 50% of the time.
- **Difference threshold / just noticeable difference (JND)**.
- **Weber's law** (E. H. Weber, 1834): the JND is a constant fraction of the original stimulus (ΔI / I = k).
- **Fechner's law** (1860): sensation grows with the logarithm of stimulus intensity.
- **Sensory adaptation** — sensitivity falls with constant stimulation (we stop smelling our own room).
- **Signal detection theory** — detection depends on sensitivity and on decision criteria.

**Kinds of senses:** vision, hearing, smell, taste, touch (skin senses: pressure, pain, warmth, cold), **kinaesthesis** (body position from muscles and joints) and the **vestibular** sense (balance). (Structure of the sense organs is in the *Nervous System* chapter.)

## 2. Perception

**Perception** (प्रत्यक्षण) is the process of organising and interpreting sensations into meaningful objects and events.

**Gestalt approach** — the **Gestalt** school (Max Wertheimer, Wolfgang Köhler, Kurt Koffka; German *Gestalt* = form, whole) is known for its explanation of **perception**: we perceive organised wholes, "the whole is different from the sum of its parts." [[PYQ: YOGA-2023DEC-P2-Q073]] Its laws of perceptual organisation:
- **figure–ground** (we separate an object from its background; the reversible vase–faces figure);
- **proximity** (near things group together), **similarity**, **closure** (we fill gaps), **continuity**, **common fate**, and the overall law of **Prägnanz** (simplest good form);
- **phi phenomenon** (apparent movement, Wertheimer 1912).

**Bottom-up vs top-down processing:**
- **Bottom-up** (data-driven): building perception from the raw features of the stimulus.
- **Top-down** (concept-driven): using **stored knowledge, expectations and schemas** to interpret new incoming information — reading a messy handwriting from context. [[PYQ: YOGA-2022DEC-P2-Q24]]

**Other perceptual topics** [[LIKELY]]:
- **Depth cues** — monocular: linear perspective, interposition (overlap), relative size, texture gradient, aerial perspective, motion parallax; binocular: **retinal disparity**, **convergence**.
- **Perceptual constancy** — size, shape, brightness, colour.
- **Illusions** — misperceptions of real stimuli (Müller-Lyer, Ponzo). (Compare hallucination: perception with *no* stimulus.)
- **Perceptual set** — readiness to perceive in a certain way.

**Agnosia** — inability to recognise despite intact senses. **Prosopagnosia** is the **inability to recognise faces despite adequate visual acuity** ("face blindness"; damage to the **fusiform face area** of the temporal–occipital cortex). [[PYQ: YOGA-2023JUN-P2-Q75]]

## 3. Attention

**Attention** (अवधान) is the selective focusing of consciousness on some stimuli while ignoring others.

- The **information-processing approach** treats the mind like a computer with a **limited capacity**: a person can perceive only certain stimuli at a time because only limited information can be taken in at once. [[PYQ: YOGA-2019DEC-P2-Q084]]
- **Filter models** [[LIKELY]]: **Broadbent's filter theory** (1958, early selection), **Treisman's attenuation theory** (1964), late-selection models; the **cocktail-party effect** (Colin Cherry, 1953: hearing your name in a noisy room).
- **Kinds**: **selective**, **divided**, **sustained** (vigilance), alternating.
- **Determinants**: *objective / external* — intensity, size, **novelty**, movement, contrast, repetition, change; *subjective / internal* — interest, motives, needs, mental set, emotion.
- **Span of attention** — about 6–7 items at a glance.
- **Yoga link**: *dhāraṇā* (concentration, YS 3.1) is sustained attention on one point; *ekāgratā* (one-pointedness) is the stage of mind suited to samādhi.

## 4. Memory

**Memory** (स्मृति) is the process of **encoding, storing and retrieving** information.

**Three stages of memory:** **encoding** (registration) → **storage** (retention / accumulation) → **retrieval** (recall, recognition). [[PYQ: YOGA-2018JUL-P2-Q56]] So the **second stage** is storage, which one question called "accumulation". [[PYQ: YOGA-2020JUN-P2-Q081]]

**Yoga and memory** — śavāsana and dhyāna improve memory because memory depends on encoding, storage and retrieval, and relaxation practices improve these functions (stress hormones impair them). [[PYQ: YOGA-2017JAN-P2-Q36]]

**Atkinson–Shiffrin multi-store model (1968):**

| Store | Capacity | Duration | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sensory memory** | large | **iconic** (visual) ~0.25–0.5 s; **echoic** (auditory) ~2–4 s | George **Sperling** (1960) partial-report experiments |
| **Short-term memory (STM)** | **7 ± 2** items (George **Miller**, 1956) | 15–30 s without rehearsal | **chunking** enlarges it; rehearsal moves items to LTM |
| **Long-term memory (LTM)** | unlimited | minutes to a lifetime | organised by meaning |

**Kinds of long-term memory** (Endel **Tulving**, 1972; Larry Squire):

- **Explicit / declarative** (conscious, "knowing that"):
  - **episodic** — personal events with time and place (my first day at college);
  - **semantic** — general facts and meanings, the **objective analysis of phenomena**, independent of when you learnt them (the capital of India). [[PYQ: YOGA-2022DEC-P2-Q23]]
- **Implicit / non-declarative** (unconscious, "knowing how"): **procedural** skills (cycling, asanas), **priming**, classical conditioning. **Implicit memory is called non-declarative memory.** [[PYQ: YOGA-2023JUN-P2-Q59]]

So the two types of **declarative** memory are **episodic** and **semantic**; iconic, echoic and short-term memory are not. [[PYQ: YOGA-2024SEP-P2-Q079]]

**Not a memory type:** "epitopic memory" — an *epitope* is the part of an antigen that an antibody binds (immunology). Real memory types: sensory, short-term, long-term, working, episodic, semantic, procedural, implicit, explicit, autobiographical, prospective. [[PYQ: YOGA-2024JUN-P2-Q071]]

**Working memory** (Baddeley and Hitch, 1974) [[LIKELY]]: **central executive**, **phonological loop**, **visuospatial sketchpad**, and (2000) the **episodic buffer**.

**Forgetting** [[LIKELY]]:
- **Hermann Ebbinghaus** (1885): nonsense syllables, the **forgetting curve** (fast loss at first, then slower), **savings** method.
- Theories: **decay** (trace fades), **interference** — **proactive** (old learning disturbs new) and **retroactive** (new disturbs old), **retrieval failure** (tip-of-the-tongue), **motivated forgetting** (repression).
- **Serial position effect**: primacy and recency.
- **Levels of processing** (Craik & Lockhart, 1972): deeper (semantic) processing gives better recall.
- **Amnesia**: **anterograde** (cannot form new memories; patient H.M., hippocampus removed) and **retrograde** (loses old memories).
- Brain: **hippocampus** consolidates declarative memory; cerebellum and basal ganglia procedural; amygdala emotional memory.

## 5. Thinking, intelligence and emotion

**Thinking** — concepts, reasoning (**deductive**: general → particular; **inductive**: particular → general), problem solving (algorithms, heuristics, insight), decision making, creativity (convergent vs divergent thinking, Guilford).

**Emotion and cognition** — cognition **is** influenced by emotion: positive mood broadens thinking and helps creative ideas (Alice Isen; Barbara Fredrickson's broaden-and-build theory). So "very good ideas come in a pleasant mood" is true, and "cognition is not influenced by emotion" is false. [[PYQ: YOGA-2019DEC-P2-Q137]]

**Theories of intelligence:**

| Theory | Who | Key idea |
|---|---|---|
| Two-factor | **Charles Spearman** (1904) | general **g** + specific **s** factors |
| Primary mental abilities | **L. L. Thurstone** (1938) | 7 abilities (verbal, number, spatial, memory, reasoning, word fluency, perceptual speed) |
| Structure of intellect | **J. P. Guilford** | operations × contents × products (up to 180 abilities) |
| Fluid and crystallised | **R. B. Cattell**, J. Horn | fluid (reasoning, declines with age) vs crystallised (knowledge) |
| **Multiple intelligences** | **Howard Gardner** (1983) | linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, musical, bodily-kinaesthetic, interpersonal, intrapersonal, naturalistic |
| **Triarchic** | **Robert Sternberg** (1985) | analytical (componential), creative (experiential), practical (contextual) |
| **Bioecological** | **Stephen Ceci** (1990) | intelligence = multiple cognitive potentials shaped by context and motivation |
| **Intelligence and cognitive development** | **Mike Anderson** (1992) | a basic processing mechanism plus specific modules that develop |
| PASS | J. P. Das, Naglieri, Kirby | **P**lanning, **A**ttention-arousal, **S**imultaneous, **S**uccessive processing |
| Emotional intelligence | Salovey & Mayer (1990); popularised by Goleman (1995) | perceiving, using, understanding, managing emotions |

[[PYQ: YOGA-2018JUL-P2-X03]]

**IQ** [[LIKELY]]: first test **Binet–Simon (1905)**; IQ = (mental age ÷ chronological age) × 100 (**William Stern**, 1912; used by **Terman** in the Stanford–Binet, 1916); Wechsler scales use deviation IQ (mean 100, SD 15).

**Theories of emotion** (संवेग):
- **James–Lange** (1884–85): bodily arousal comes first; the emotion *is* our perception of it ("we are afraid because we tremble").
- **Cannon–Bard** (1927): the **thalamus** triggers bodily arousal and felt emotion **at the same time**.

Both are theories of emotion. [[PYQ: YOGA-2024SEP-P2-Q057]] Also [[LIKELY]]: **Schachter–Singer two-factor** theory (1962: arousal + cognitive label), **Lazarus**'s cognitive appraisal theory, Plutchik's wheel of eight primary emotions, the **limbic system** (amygdala, hypothalamus) as the brain's emotion circuit, and the **Yerkes–Dodson law** (performance best at moderate arousal).

**Motivation** — drives and needs (Hull's drive reduction), arousal theory, incentive theory, **Maslow's hierarchy** (see *Personality*), intrinsic vs extrinsic motivation, achievement motivation (McClelland).
